01 / THE MACHINE

WHAT WAS OPPORTUNITY?

A ROBOTIC GEOLOGIST

Opportunity was a solar-powered robotic rover developed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. It was part of the Mars Exploration Rover mission, alongside its twin rover, Spirit.

Opportunity landed in the Meridiani Planum region of Mars in January 2004. Its primary objective was to examine rocks and soil for geological evidence of ancient water.

It was designed to travel approximately one kilometre and operate for 90 Martian days. Instead, it travelled more than 45 kilometres and operated for over 14 years.

MISSION OBJECTIVES

  • Search for geological evidence of ancient water.
  • Study the composition of Martian rocks and soil.
  • Investigate craters and exposed rock layers.
  • Examine minerals formed in the presence of water.
  • Demonstrate long-duration robotic surface exploration.
02 / ENGINEERING

INSIDE THE ROVER

Opportunity was considerably larger and more capable than the Sojourner rover. It carried a collection of scientific instruments mounted on a mobile robotic platform.

01

SIX-WHEEL MOBILITY

Six wheels and a rocker-bogie suspension system allowed Opportunity to travel across uneven Martian terrain, climb slopes, and approach scientific targets.

02

PANCAM

The panoramic camera system captured detailed colour images of the surrounding landscape. These images helped scientists study the geology and plan the rover's route.

03

APXS

The Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer measured the elemental composition of rocks and soil, helping scientists identify their chemical characteristics.

04

MINI-TES

The Miniature Thermal Emission Spectrometer studied infrared radiation from rocks and soil to identify minerals and investigate their composition.

05

ROCK ABRASION TOOL

The Rock Abrasion Tool, or RAT, ground away the outer surface of selected rocks so that scientific instruments could examine relatively fresh material beneath.

06

SOLAR POWER

Solar panels converted sunlight into electrical energy. Rechargeable batteries stored energy for operations when sunlight was unavailable.

03 / SCIENTIFIC DISCOVERIES

TRACES OF AN ANCIENT MARS

Opportunity's most important contribution was its evidence that liquid water had affected the Martian environment in the distant past.

THE MARTIAN BLUEBERRIES

Near its landing site, Opportunity discovered small, rounded mineral formations embedded in the rocks. These formations became known informally as Martian blueberries.

Their composition and geological context provided evidence of past water-related processes. They were an important clue in reconstructing the history of Mars.

WATER-ALTERED ROCKS

Opportunity examined rocks containing minerals and textures associated with interaction with liquid water.

Its observations showed that parts of Mars had once experienced wet environmental conditions. Some of these environments may have been suitable for microbial life.

04 / MISSION CHRONOLOGY

FIFTEEN YEARS ON MARS

From its launch in 2003 to its final communication in 2018, Opportunity travelled across the Martian landscape and investigated numerous geological sites.

07 JULY 2003 — LAUNCH

Opportunity launched from Cape Canaveral, Florida, aboard a Delta II rocket. It began its journey to Mars as part of NASA's Mars Exploration Rover mission.

JANUARY 2004 — LANDING

Opportunity landed in Meridiani Planum inside a small impact crater later named Eagle Crater. It began examining the surrounding rocks and soil.

2004 — ENDURANCE CRATER

After exploring Eagle Crater, Opportunity travelled to Endurance Crater. It examined exposed rock layers and gathered evidence about the region's geological history.

2005 — THE HEAT SHIELD ROCK

Near its discarded heat shield, Opportunity discovered a rock identified as an iron-nickel meteorite. This was the first meteorite identified on another planet.

2006–2011 — EXTENDED EXPLORATION

Opportunity continued travelling through the Martian landscape, investigating Victoria Crater and eventually reaching the much larger Endeavour Crater.

25 MARCH 2015 — MARATHON DISTANCE

Opportunity travelled 42.195 kilometres, becoming the first human-made vehicle to complete a marathon distance on the surface of another world.

2018 — PERSEVERANCE VALLEY

Opportunity continued exploring the western edge of Endeavour Crater. Its final scientific work took place in the region known as Perseverance Valley.

10 JUNE 2018 — LAST COMMUNICATION

A severe planet-wide dust storm reduced the sunlight reaching Opportunity's solar panels. The rover sent its final communication to Earth.

13 FEBRUARY 2019 — MISSION ENDS

After months of unsuccessful attempts to restore contact, NASA officially declared the Opportunity mission complete.

05 / THE FINAL SIGNAL

WHY DID OPPORTUNITY STOP?

LOST BENEATH THE DUST

Opportunity depended on sunlight to generate electrical power through its solar panels. During June 2018, a severe dust storm spread across Mars and covered much of the planet.

Dust in the atmosphere blocked sunlight from reaching the rover's solar panels. As available electrical energy decreased, Opportunity could no longer maintain normal operations and communication with Earth.

Its final communication was received on 10 June 2018. Engineers sent more than a thousand commands in an attempt to re-establish contact, but the rover never responded again.

NASA officially ended the mission on 13 February 2019. The exact sequence of hardware failures after the loss of communication was not directly observed.

Opportunity remains on Mars, in the region of Perseverance Valley. Its silence marked the end of a mission that had lasted far beyond its original design.

06 / THE LEGACY

A JOURNEY BEYOND EXPECTATIONS

AN ENGINEERING ACHIEVEMENT

Opportunity demonstrated that a robotic explorer could operate on another planet for many years, despite harsh environmental conditions, hardware failures, and communication challenges.

Its 45.16-kilometre journey remains a major milestone in planetary exploration.

A SCIENTIFIC LEGACY

Its discoveries transformed our understanding of ancient Mars by showing that liquid water had once played an important role in shaping the planet's surface.

Opportunity helped establish the scientific and engineering foundation for later Mars exploration missions.
